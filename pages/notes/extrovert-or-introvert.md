This is a fascinating psychological scenario, and the short answer is that **the person could absolutely be considered an Introvert, even while exhibiting those behaviors.**

The confusion lies in the difference between _behavior_ (what you do) and _energy source_ (where you draw your strength).

Here is a breakdown of why this contradiction is possible, using the standard definitions of personality:

---

### 1. Defining Introversion vs. Extroversion

It is crucial to understand that most modern psychological models (like the Big Five) define introversion and extroversion not as "shyness" or "sociability," but as where a person gains and expends energy.

- **Extroverts:** Draw energy from the outside world, from social interaction, stimulation, and external activity. They tend to talk more easily and gain energy from being around people.
- **Introverts:** Draw energy from the inside world, from solitary reflection, deep thought, and quiet spaces. Social interaction, especially prolonged or high-intensity interaction, tends to **drain** their energy reserves.
- **Ambiverts:** Fall somewhere in the middle, depending on the situation and their needs.

### 2. Analyzing the Behavior

The behavior you described—initiating conversations, rambling, asking personal questions (laptop brands, living situations, children, work)—is high-energy, external, and highly engaging. _On the surface_, this looks like textbook extroverted behavior.

**However, the fact that they _self-identified_ as an introvert is the key piece of information.**

### 3. Reconciliation: How an Introvert Acts Like an Extrovert

There are several ways someone who self-identifies as an introvert can still act outwardly in a manner that appears extroverted:

#### A. Social Competence (Skill vs. Need)

Many introverts are highly **socially skilled**. They can entertain conversations, ask engaging questions, and perform socially without needing the activity to _recharge_ them. They may be good at small talk, but the conversation, once over, still leaves them feeling drained because their brain expended energy on external input rather than internal processing.

- **Analogy:** A professional wrestler can perform a high-energy, extrovert-like performance, but off-stage, they need complete solitude to recover.

#### B. The High-Energy Introvert

Some introverts are simply very engaged by the world. They can be curious, talkative, and passionate about the topics they bring up. Their outward engagement doesn't mean they get energy from the social stimulus; it means they are highly attuned to the _topic_ or the _moment_, even if the interaction itself is ultimately taxing.

#### C. The Ambivert

This is the most likely possibility. Ambiverts are truly in the middle. They enjoy social interaction, can be quite vocal, and might initiate conversations, but they also absolutely need significant downtime to recover, and they might feel awkward or overstimulated by too much of it. They are not pure introverts or pure extroverts.

#### D. The Performance (The "Social Mask")

It is possible the person was in a "performance mode" during the conversation. They may be social enough to be engaging and curious because they are naturally inclined to be pleasant in public, but the underlying need for quiet solitude remains.

### Conclusion

**Would they be considered an extrovert?** Based purely on the behavior, they presented very extroverted traits.

**Could they still be an introvert?** Yes, absolutely. The key distinction is whether the behavior is driven by an _need for social stimulation_ (extroversion) or merely _social competence/curiosity_ that requires recovery time (introversion/ambiverting).

If they felt drained afterward or needed immediate solitude to "reset" after the interaction, they are almost certainly an introvert or ambivert.
