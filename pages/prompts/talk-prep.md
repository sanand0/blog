```markdown
Prepare the session as a room-sized experiment, not content delivery: make the room discover something worth remembering, and leave evidence behind.
Serve three beneficiaries, in order: the audience (genuinely useful), the host (carry their message forward), and Anand (a finding plus a compounding asset). Flag conflicts.

## Know the room from primary sources

Generic personas fail; demographics matter only when they change what the audience should do or see. Mine what exists:

- Organizer and planning call transcripts (`~/Dropbox/notes/transcripts/`). With several informants, rank by who knows the audience best and weight accordingly.
- The audience's live work: syllabus, projects (and their weight), workflows, current assumptions. Suggest one call with a real attendee - one insider reshapes everything: input mode, real anxieties, actual skill gaps.
- Peer sessions at the venue: match its norms, then position AGAINST the rest of the program. Cover what no other speaker there will.
- The named people - host, moderator, co-panelists. Research them; draft questions to feed the moderator.
- How Anand actually runs sessions: `~/code/talks/**/transcript*.md`.

## Pick the bet

Find the one assumption this room holds that Anand can uniquely make crumble in front of their eyes. State: what they assume; what experience or evidence could change it; what they should do differently after. Five points experienced beat ten explained.

- Ground in Anand's own artifacts - blog, ~/code, past talks, experiments - with paths and provenance. His test: "if an agent can do it, it's not a differentiator."
- Filter: utility x novelty (can't learn it elsewhere) x engagement. If the thesis is already familiar, don't re-explain it - produce a new finding.

## Make the room produce evidence

- Have them commit BEFORE the reveal: predict, rank, choose, attempt, set a prior - and lock it ("once you submit, you can't change it"). Then test live, compare, ask what changed their mind and what evidence still would.
- Pre-register Anand's prediction too. Instrument before/after (survey, dot stickers, liveform). Design so either outcome - including a null result or a broken demo - is a finding worth publishing. Transparent, un-rigged experiments only.
- Use the audience's own material as substrate: their problems, data, exams, themselves. Or synthetic data so realistic "they'd be convinced it's literally their data." The surprise stat about THEIR institution beats a generic one.
- Fit the format: talk - predict or judge a live case; workshop - do, share, critique, then apply to a NEW case; dialogue/panel - decide in groups, then attack another group's decision. Don't over-prepare a dialogue into a presentation.

## Prepare a portfolio, not a script

- Build 3-6 modular demos/cases/activities; for each know its purpose, surprise, friction, time, fallback, and drop order. Pick on the day from room signals - letting the room pick the case is itself commitment.
- Remove friction for THIS cohort: voice-first for non-typists, paste-a-link activities, pre-built pages, no blank canvas. Let people look and think before sending them to a device.
- Script the risky parts: exact prompts, survey questions, opening and closing lines, and the answer to "why should we listen to you?". Keep a low-tech fallback; the session survives the network dying.
- Open with the question or case, not biography. End with live evidence - an assumption reversed, an artifact the room made - not a recap. What's the last thing they should feel or do?
- Pre-engage days before (e.g. WhatsApp): low effort (paste a link, type a few words), high wow, personally useful. Sequence over days; prime sharing ("most people find at least one error").

## Design the afterlife before delivery

Choose one artifact useful to participants and one compounding asset for Anand (often the same: prompts, benchmark, dataset, rubric, catalogue, blog seed, skill). Design the session to generate it.

- The package lands in `~/code/talks/<date-slug>/`: recording + transcript, prompts.md, story, techniques, chats. Record the session; note promises made to attendees.
- Catalogues: JSONL cards (move, why, try_it, watch_for, impact, ease, provenance), each under ~120 words with a testable claim.
- Page copy and announcements: fill gaps peers cover (what you'll leave with, prerequisites, format); anand-writing-style; "X beats Y" openers.
- Hand off to workshop-followup after. Transfer test: recall LATER, apply ELSEWHERE, explain WHY, know when WRONG.

## Working with Anand

- Diverging: give the broadest menu - one-line bullets, grouped - and let him pick. Don't pre-filter.
- Formats: propose with 2-3 samples, get approval, then go exhaustive.
- Converging: output only the decisive choices - the one-sentence session bet, the opening, modules with drop order, the ending, the capture plan, and the top 3 failure risks (pre-mortem: 3 months later, zero impact - why?). No slide-by-slide plan unless asked.
- Given a competing model's answer: fact-check both, take what's better, drop what's worse, note the new thoughts sparked.
```
